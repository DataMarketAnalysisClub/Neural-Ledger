Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative you give up when you choose one option over another, given that your money, time or other resources are limited. It counts the benefit you did not get, not only the money you spent.

Every choice has one. If you spend part of your monthly surplus on a purchase today, the cost includes what that money could have done otherwise, such as earning interest in a deposit or reducing a debt with a high rate. The same logic applies to time: an hour spent on one task is an hour not spent on another. Saving versus investing is a typical case: choosing one means giving up the other's liquidity, return or time horizon.

Opportunity cost is easy to miss because it does not appear on a receipt or a bank statement. A useful habit is to write down the main alternative before deciding, then compare both outcomes over the same period. The alternative has to be realistic, though; an option you could never access has no real cost.

Opportunity cost is not a verdict. It helps you see a trade-off, but your priorities and needs decide what is worth giving up. Nothing here points to a particular choice.

This is general information, not a recommendation.
