A real amount is adjusted for changes in prices, so it shows purchasing power, while a nominal amount is simply the figure as written on the day. A nominal interest rate is the rate a bank or contract states; the real rate is what remains after subtracting inflation.

A simple way to see it: if a deposit pays a nominal rate and prices rise by about the same percentage, the real return is close to zero, even though the balance grew. Economists describe the link with an approximation known as the Fisher relation: the nominal rate is roughly the real rate plus expected inflation.

The distinction matters when comparing options over several years. A higher nominal figure can still buy less if inflation was higher, and fixed nominal payments lose value in real terms as prices rise. Adjusting for inflation, for example by using UF amounts, is one way to make the comparison fair.

Official inflation data comes from the INE, and the Banco Central publishes its own expectations and analysis about future inflation.

This is general information, not a recommendation.
