A real amount is adjusted for changes in prices, so it shows purchasing power, while a nominal amount is simply the figure as written on the day. A nominal interest rate is the rate a bank or contract states; the real rate is what remains after subtracting inflation.

For example, if an account pays 1% nominal interest and inflation is 3%, the real rate is roughly minus 2%: the balance grows, but what it can buy falls. Economists summarize this relation with an approximation known as the Fisher relation: the nominal rate is roughly the real rate plus expected inflation.

The difference matters when comparing options over several years. A higher nominal figure can still buy less if inflation was higher, and fixed peso payments lose real value as prices rise. Adjusting for inflation, for example by expressing amounts in UF, makes the comparison fairer.

Official inflation figures are published on the INE website, so any stated rate can be checked against that data.

This is general information, not a recommendation.
