Compound interest is interest earned on both the original amount and the interest already added to it. Because each period's earnings join the balance, the next period's interest is calculated on a larger base, so the total grows faster than it would with a fixed amount of interest.

A small example shows the effect. Suppose you leave CLP 100,000 in an account for 2 years at a 5% annual rate, with interest added once a year. At the end of the two years the balance is CLP 110,250. With simple interest, the same deposit would reach CLP 110,000. The gap looks small here, but it widens as the years add up.

Three factors decide how much compounding matters: the rate, the length of time, and how often interest is added. For the same stated rate, more frequent additions usually leave a higher final balance.

Compounding also works against borrowers. A debt that accrues interest and is not paid can grow the same way, which is why compound interest matters when you use credit cards and loans, not only when you save. In Chile, check how each product calculates interest before comparing offers.

This is general information, not a recommendation.
