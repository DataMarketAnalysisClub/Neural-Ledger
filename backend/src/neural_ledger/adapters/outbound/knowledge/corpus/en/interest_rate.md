An interest rate is the price of using someone else's money, expressed as a percentage of the amount borrowed or saved over a period, usually one year.

When you take a loan, the rate tells you how much you repay on top of the principal. The lender applies it to the balance still outstanding, so the same rate costs more when the debt is larger or stays open longer. Lenders set it to cover the risk that a borrower does not pay, the effect of inflation, and what the money could earn somewhere else.

Rates come in two forms. A fixed rate stays the same for the whole contract. A variable rate moves with a reference index plus a margin that the lender adds, so your payments can change. Check which one applies before you sign.

The headline rate is not the full price. Fees, insurance and compounding can raise what you actually pay, which is why Chile uses the CAE to compare credits. Also look at the rate charged on late payments, which is often higher. In Chile, the CMF publishes average rates and sets a legal ceiling called the tasa máxima convencional (TMC). The TMC changes over time, so check cmfchile.cl for current values rather than relying on any figure you read elsewhere.

This is general information, not a recommendation.
