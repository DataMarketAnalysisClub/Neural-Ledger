A bond is a loan that an investor makes to an issuer, such as a government or a company. In return, the issuer usually pays interest on set dates and repays the original amount on a fixed date called maturity.

A bond differs from a stock. The holder is a lender, not an owner, so a bond does not carry a share of profits or a vote in decisions. If the issuer runs into trouble, lenders are paid before shareholders, but not all lenders rank equally, so repayment is never assured.

The main risks are three. Interest rate risk: when market rates rise, an older bond paying a lower fixed rate usually loses market value, so selling before maturity can mean a loss. Credit risk: the issuer may be unable to pay interest or return the principal. Inflation risk: rising prices can shrink what the payments are worth in real terms.

In Chile, the state and companies issue bonds in pesos or in UF, a unit linked to inflation. These products are regulated by the CMF (Comisión para el Mercado Financiero). Terms and credit ratings differ widely between issuers, so the features of any bond are set out in its own prospectus.
