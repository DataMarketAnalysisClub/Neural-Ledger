A bond is a loan that an investor makes to an issuer, such as a government or a company. In return, the issuer usually pays interest at set intervals and repays the original amount on a fixed date, called maturity.

A bond differs from a stock. The holder is a lender, not an owner, so a bond does not give a share of the company's profits or a vote in its decisions. If the issuer runs into trouble, lenders are paid before shareholders, but secured creditors usually come first, so repayment is never assured.

The main risks are these. Interest rate risk: when market rates rise, an existing bond paying a lower fixed rate tends to lose value, so selling before maturity can mean a loss. Credit risk: the issuer may be unable to pay interest or return the principal. Inflation can also reduce what the payments are worth in real terms.

In Chile, bonds are issued by the state and by companies, often in pesos or in UF, a unit linked to inflation. Terms and credit ratings differ widely between issuers, so the features of any bond depend on its own prospectus.
